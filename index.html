Duck Dash: River Run

My role: gameplay design and the trailer

My part

Keeping every stage surprising

I designed and built the gameplay systems that stop players from settling in, then cut the trailer that sells it.

  • Seven hazards, one at a time.Homing mines, turrets that aim where you're going, police boats lying in ambush, tornadoes, giant waves, blackouts and earthquakes.
  • A duck you can't memorise.Admiral Quack's timing shifts every round, he turns faster on boss stages, and he fakes a turn to catch anyone who flinches early.
  • Coins that keep you guessing.Seven coin patterns, picked at random each run, and a bot I built to prove every coin can always be reached.
  • The trailer.Real gameplay, scripted camera shots and slow-motion moments, cut to music in 15 seconds.
Team effort

Crafting the fun

The Best Animation award belongs to my teammates' craft: the duck's dramatic turn, the cinematic jump between worlds where the duck pops and the river morphs into the next theme, and the sounds and haptics that make every near-miss feel tense. Each of the six worlds has its own lighting, weather, colours and sound.

With AI speeding up the building, the whole team had more time to pour into details like these.

Economics

Designing for players

Economics taught me that people respond to incentives, and a game is really an incentive system with art on top. Here's how we designed for that.

“A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.”

Herbert Simon, Nobel laureate in economics

Bring them in

  • Loud on first sight. A bold title and a cinematic trailer that sell the game in seconds.
  • A rule everyone already knows. Squid Game made Red Light, Green Light famous worldwide. Now the show has ended and the hype has moved on, so players get the rules instantly and we bet there was room to bring it back.

Keep them playing

  • Feel first. Satisfying haptics and loud, splashy water for an ASMR-like kick on every move.
  • A finish worth reaching. Cross the line and the duck bursts in a glorious explosion, a payoff so gratifying that players stick around to see what's next.
  • Achievements that add up. 130 of them, so the more you earn, the more you'd leave behind: a sunk cost that keeps players invested.
  • Progress you can see. Stages get steadily harder while free boats and trails unlock along the way, so players feel themselves moving up.
Perspective

What I learned as a player

The games I stuck with never forced me to pay, so spending felt like supporting something I loved rather than paying to skip frustration. And Call of Duty: Mobile (published here by Garena, my first taste of the company's games, and one I still play today) showed me that the strongest pull is other people: every friend who joined gave me another reason to log back in.

Personal project · Experiment

Automated sleep tracker

Over a month of nights, measured while I slept.

The question

I'd started to wonder whether I might have sleep apnea. I didn't want to jump to conclusions, and I didn't want to ignore it either. So I set out to measure my own sleep: not to diagnose myself, but as an early warning. I wanted a baseline of how my nights really look, and to know whether anything was worth a doctor's attention.

I was careful about what a phone on a bedside table can and can't tell you. This was never a clinical study, so every result is treated as a clue, not a conclusion. What I was after was reassurance, real information about my own health, and the chance to spot any patterns worth following up.

The plan

Two phases. First, collect: record every night and file the numbers, with no conclusions allowed. Then, once there was enough data, analyse: look for patterns across the whole archive and check them against real research.

How it works

  1. 1Record

    My iPhone and a secondary microphone record the night from beside the bed.

  2. 2Analyse

    One double-click and my tool finds every snore, movement and silence, checks for fan or aircon noise that could hide sounds, and draws the night as a chart.

  3. 3File

    I type /sleep in a Claude chat, and AI files the night alongside the day before (caffeine, alcohol, meals, rest), then replies in one line.

  4. 4Research

    Once a few weeks of results were in, AI research agents searched the medical literature in twelve parallel lanes, cross-checking 117 sources, so every finding rests on well-supported evidence.

What I took away

Measure before you guess. Make the boring part automatic, or it won't happen every day. Let the evidence overrule you. And when something seems impossible to find out on your own, try anyway.

A personal experiment, not a medical test. The results stay between me and my pillow.

Personal project · First trips

Campus bus planner

For every first trip to somewhere new on a very big campus.

The problem

NUS is huge. On my first trip to a new lecture hall, I don't know which shuttle goes there, where to get off, or how far the walk is.

What I built

So I built a planner. I type in the room, and it tells me which bus to catch, when it's really coming, where to hop off and how long I'll walk. It knows over 1,000 rooms and buildings across all 33 stops.

Was it overkill?

For getting to class? Probably a little. But it was the perfect excuse to experiment, and building the extra thing is how I find my next idea.

Personal project · Curiosity

Case Closed

The case files of a curious mind.

Why I built it

I learn best when I'm hooked, and nothing hooks me like a mystery. So I built a storyteller. I give it a topic, or let it surprise me, and AI turns a real piece of history or science into a case I have to crack myself.

How a case works

The story comes one stop at a time, and it never just hands me the answer. At every stop I have to read the clues and commit to a theory before the story moves on. Every case is a workout for my inductive reasoning: spotting the pattern before the reveal.

My favourite case

The Homeric Question

Ancient Greece had two poems as long as novels, made when almost no one could write. How? Scholars argued for 2,000 years. The clue was hiding in plain sight: phrases like “swift-footed Achilles” repeat again and again. Singers remembered the story, not the exact words. These ready-made phrases fit the rhythm perfectly, so they kept the song going while the singer thought of the next line. Every performance was rebuilt live, and there was never one fixed text.

Other cases I've cracked: a DNA “killer” who turned out to be a cotton-swab factory worker, how sailors found the cure for scurvy and then lost it again, and how one chemist finally pinned down the age of the Earth.

Keeping it

When a case closes, AI turns it into a page I can reread: the story in brief, plus every question I answered along the way. It turns curiosity into something I actually remember.
